Multi-Unit and Commercial Countertop Installation

By the Editorial Team | Published: September 2026 | Last Updated: September 2026

What Distinguishes Multi-Unit From Single-Unit Work

Multi-unit and commercial countertop installations involve fundamentally different structural relationships than residential projects. The client is typically a developer, property manager, or construction company rather than an individual homeowner. The end-users are multiple parties who will eventually occupy the units. The design decisions are made once and applied across many units. The coordination structure involves multiple parties across an extended construction schedule. Understanding these structural differences is essential for anyone entering this scale of work, whether as a developer commissioning it or a fabricator delivering it.

The transition from large residential to multi-unit or commercial work is not just a scale increase; it is a shift in the underlying project shape. Bringing residential process to a multi-unit project produces frustrating outcomes for both the developer (who expects commercial-grade documentation) and the fabricator (who is not set up for the volume). Understanding when residential process ends and multi-unit process begins is the first step to structuring these projects correctly.

Typical Characteristics of Multi-Unit Projects

Multi-unit projects share characteristics that distinguish them from residential work.

  • Multiple end-users: the installed counters will be used by different parties (residents, tenants, customers) rather than one homeowner.
  • Repeated identical or similar specifications: the same counter design is often repeated across many units with minor variations. This enables production efficiency but requires standardization discipline.
  • Formal contract structure: contracts include specifications, delivery schedules, payment milestones, and typically performance requirements.
  • Extended construction timeline: the project may run for 3 to 12 months or longer, with counter installation as one phase of the broader construction schedule.
  • Multiple installation windows: installation typically happens in phases as units become ready rather than all at once.
  • Formal change management: changes require written change orders and typically involve multiple parties in the approval process.
  • Coordination through GC or construction manager: the developer typically works through a general contractor or construction manager who coordinates all trades including the fabricator.

Typical Timeline for Multi-Unit Projects

Multi-unit timelines are longer than residential and structured differently. Instead of a compressed 4-to-6-week sequence, multi-unit projects run for months with distinct phases.

Phase Typical duration Key activities
Design and specification 4 to 8 weeks Design finalized, samples approved, master specifications documented
Contract and prep 2 to 4 weeks Contract negotiated, material sourced, production capacity reserved
Sample and mockup 2 to 4 weeks Physical sample unit fabricated and installed for developer approval
Staged production Ongoing throughout construction Fabrication runs aligned to construction schedule
Staged installation Ongoing across units Installation as units reach counter-ready state
Punch list and closeout 2 to 4 weeks after final unit Warranty documentation, final inspections, project closeout

Notice that fabrication and installation happen in overlapping ongoing phases rather than as sequential steps. The project runs continuously across the construction schedule rather than as discrete stages.

Coordination Structure at Multi-Unit Scale

Coordination at multi-unit scale is fundamentally different from residential.

The GC or Construction Manager as Coordinator

On most multi-unit projects, the fabricator does not coordinate directly with the developer. A general contractor or construction manager holds the master schedule and coordinates the fabricator with all other trades. Communication typically flows through the GC rather than directly to the developer.

Formal Meeting Structure

Multi-unit projects typically use structured meeting cadence: weekly or bi-weekly project meetings, formal status reports, documented action items. The informality of residential communication is replaced by formal process.

Multiple Parallel Threads

Multi-unit projects run multiple parallel threads: some units are being measured while others are being installed while others are in punch list. The coordination structure needs to track all threads simultaneously rather than a single linear sequence.

Change Control

Changes require formal change orders with cost and schedule impact documented and approved by the developer or construction manager. The ad hoc changes common in residential work are not typically accommodated.

Fabricator Requirements for Multi-Unit Work

Multi-unit work requires fabricator capabilities that many residential-focused fabricators do not have.

  • Production capacity for continuous fabrication: the shop needs to produce pieces continuously across months without capacity conflicts with residential work.
  • Formal project management: dedicated project managers who handle the coordination through the GC or construction manager.
  • Contract capacity: the fabricator can negotiate and execute formal commercial contracts with milestones, performance requirements, and change control.
  • Insurance and bonding: commercial projects typically require insurance limits and sometimes performance bonds beyond what residential work needs.
  • Multiple installation crews: the fabricator can deploy multiple crews to install units as they become ready, rather than being limited to sequential single-crew work.
  • Quality control at scale: the fabricator has processes to ensure consistency across units when producing many similar counters.

Fabricators without these capabilities can sometimes take on smaller multi-unit projects, but scaling up beyond that is typically not feasible without developing the underlying capabilities.

Sample and Mockup Discipline

A specific feature of multi-unit work that residential projects do not typically use is the sample and mockup phase. Before full production begins, the fabricator produces a sample unit that is installed and reviewed by the developer. Approval of the sample commits both parties to the specification and reduces the risk of finding problems after many units have been produced.

The sample discipline is time-consuming (typically 2 to 4 weeks) but valuable because it catches problems before they multiply across units. Skipping this phase to save time on multi-unit projects almost always produces more expensive problems later.

Common Multi-Unit Project Failure Modes

Some failures come up repeatedly on multi-unit projects.

Insufficient material sourcing at contract: the fabricator commits to a specific stone without confirming enough slabs are available for the entire program. Running short mid-project produces expensive scrambles.

Schedule creep across the construction program: if the broader construction is delayed, the counter schedule needs to adjust. Fabricators without flexibility can produce mismatched delivery cycles.

Quality variation across units: without deliberate quality control, later units can differ subtly from earlier units in ways that residents notice when comparing.

Communication gaps between parties: the developer, GC, and fabricator all need to align on decisions. Gaps between these parties produce conflicting instructions or missed deadlines.

Change order backlog: changes accumulate faster than they are formally processed, and later work proceeds without proper documentation of what has changed.

Cost Structure for Multi-Unit Work

Multi-unit pricing is structured differently from residential. Typical structures include per-unit pricing (each unit's counter costs a fixed amount regardless of specific area), per-square-foot pricing at scale (lower rates than residential due to production efficiency), or fixed-price program contracts with performance milestones.

Payment structures also differ. Multi-unit contracts often have milestone-based payment schedules (payment triggered by specific deliverables) rather than the deposit-plus-completion structure common in residential. Understanding both the pricing and payment structure at contract signing prevents cash-flow surprises.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the smallest multi-unit project size?

Approximately 10 units is where multi-unit process starts to make sense. Projects with 3 to 9 similar units often use residential process with some adaptations. Projects with 10 or more units typically benefit from formal multi-unit process.

Can a residential fabricator handle a multi-unit project?

Small ones, sometimes. A 15-unit residential development can be handled by a well-run residential fabricator with growth capacity. Larger programs typically require fabricators with dedicated multi-unit capabilities.

How does the developer know a fabricator can handle multi-unit work?

By checking specific portfolio of similar-scale multi-unit projects, verifying insurance and bonding capacity, and evaluating whether the fabricator has dedicated project management for commercial work. Fabricators without commercial track records typically struggle with the underlying business processes.

Do multi-unit projects use different materials than residential?

Sometimes. Multi-unit projects need materials available in the quantities required across the program. This filters out rare or specialty stones and favors materials with predictable supply.

How much do sample and mockup phases add to the timeline?

Typically 2 to 4 weeks. This phase is time-consuming but valuable because it catches problems before they multiply across units.

What happens if a change needs to be made mid-program?

Change orders are documented, priced, and approved through the formal process. Small changes typically process within a week or two; larger changes may require more extensive review. The formal process is slower than residential ad hoc changes but produces the documentation needed for the program.

Does the developer coordinate directly with the fabricator?

Sometimes, but often through a GC or construction manager. Coordination structure depends on the project's overall management approach. Fabricators should confirm the communication structure at contract signing.

What are the biggest risks specific to multi-unit work?

Material supply consistency across the program, quality consistency across units, and schedule alignment with the broader construction. All three risks are managed through disciplined process rather than eliminated. Fabricators without these processes face higher risk of program failures.
